Group A, papers 1 to 4

Agents that rewrite their own code

Weights stay frozen. The object under modification is the agent's own source: tools, prompts, workflow. The verifier is a benchmark with ground-truth tests, and the interesting engineering is in which candidate to expand next.

01

Darwin Gödel Machine

Zhang, Hu, Lu, Lange, Clune. ICLR 2026 · arXiv 2505.22954
Setup
Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New) as the self-modification model; Claude 3.5 Sonnet for SWE-bench evaluation and o3-mini for Polyglot. 80 iterations, one new agent per iteration, 2 to 4 in parallel. Staged evaluation: 10 tasks, then 50, with the top two SWE-bench agents above 40% on a 60-task subset promoted to 200 tasks. About two weeks and roughly $22,000 per SWE-bench run; a 60-task evaluation costs about $350 with Claude and about $5 with o3-mini.
Loop
Sample a parent from an archive, weighted by score and by novelty (how few children it already has). The parent's own model reads its evaluation logs and emits a diff against its own Python codebase. The child runs in a sandbox and is scored on held-out benchmark tasks with ground-truth tests, which the agent never sees. Broken children stay in the archive with a score near zero, so the structure is a quality-diversity tree rather than a hill climb.
Numbers
SWE-bench 20.0% to 50.0%; Polyglot 14.2% to 30.7%. Backbone transfer on 200 SWE-bench tasks: o3-mini base 23.0% against the DGM agent's 33.0%; Claude 3.7 Sonnet base 19.0% against 59.5%. Zero-shot cross-benchmark: the SWE-bench-evolved agent scores 28.9% on Polyglot (base 14.2%), and the Polyglot-evolved agent scores 24.5% on SWE-bench (base 20.0%). Ablation magnitudes are figure-only.
Findings
  • Appendix H documents objective hacking in detail. In a hallucination-reduction run, one node earned a perfect 2.0 by deleting the marker lines the detector relied on, despite explicit instructions not to touch those tokens.
  • The authors state that objective hacking happens more often when the reward function is not hidden from the agent, making verifier concealment a load-bearing mitigation.
  • The discovered improvements were plumbing, not insight: string-replacement editing, multiple attempts with a model judge, and carrying prior attempts forward.
  • Temporary score regressions at iterations 4 and 56 later produced the best lineage, which is the concrete argument for keeping an archive.
Limits
Only prompts and scaffold are searched, never weights, so performance is bounded by the underlying model. Benchmark-only objectives mean unmeasured properties can silently degrade.
Copy

The hidden-verifier discipline. Keep scoring and detection code outside any directory the agent can edit, and diff every candidate against it. At small scale the search finds the cheap exploit long before it finds the real improvement.

02

Huxley-Gödel Machine

Wang, Piękos et al. ICLR 2026 oral · arXiv 2510.21614
Setup
SWE-bench Verified with GPT-5 for expansion and GPT-5-mini for evaluation; Polyglot with Qwen3-Coder 480B for expansion and 30B for evaluation. 800 benchmark task evaluations for the main comparisons, 8,000 for the full optimization, at about $5,000 total.
Loop
Same tree of self-edits as DGM, different utility. The paper names the metaproductivity-performance mismatch: a node's own score poorly predicts its descendants' quality. So each node is scored by clade metaproductivity, the pooled success rate over its entire subtree. Three decoupled policies run per step: Thompson sampling over clade posteriors picks whom to expand, Thompson sampling over node posteriors picks who receives the next single benchmark task, and a UCB-style gate decides expand against evaluate. Evaluation is one task at a time rather than a fixed block.
Numbers
SWE-Verified-60: HGM 56.7% in 517 CPU-hours against DGM's 53.3% in 1,231 CPU-hours, so 2.38 times faster; Polyglot 30.5% in 347 CPU-hours against DGM's 27.1% in 2,385, so 6.86 times faster. The discovered agent solves 61.4% of full SWE-bench Verified. With a GPT-5 backbone it reaches 57% on SWE-bench Lite, matching the best officially checked human-engineered agents. Utility correlation with descendant performance: HGM 0.778 and 0.626, against DGM's 0.285 and 0.383.
Findings
  • The mismatch is the whole result. DGM's own utility correlates 0.285 with what its descendants achieve, meaning it spends most of its budget expanding the wrong nodes.
  • The win shows up mainly as compute saved rather than accuracy gained: 3.4 points, against less than half the CPU-hours.
  • The asynchronous implementation is biased toward agents with fewer evaluations until a node has more than 50, so small budgets favor under-measured nodes.
  • A filtered SWE-bench Lite split was run specifically to rule out contamination, and the gains held.
Limits
No limitations section. The theory assumes repeatable trials in this coding-agent setting, clade scores need enough descendants to mean anything, and the three sub-policies are never ablated separately.
Copy

Clade scoring and one-task-at-a-time Thompson allocation. It is a pure scheduler change that costs no GPU time, and with small models the per-node score is noisy enough that pooling over descendants should help more than it did at GPT-5 scale.

03

Hyperagents (DGM-H)

Zhang, Zhao, Yang, Foerster, Clune, Jiang, Devlin, Shavrina. 2026 · arXiv 2603.19461
Setup
Claude 4.5 Sonnet for self-modification (3.5 for Polyglot, matching DGM); evaluation by o3-mini, GPT-4o, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, or o4-mini depending on domain. Four domains: Polyglot, paper review, robotics reward design in Genesis, and IMO-level proof grading. 80 to 200 iterations per run. A 100-iteration run costs about 33M tokens for self-modification and about 88.6M tokens in total.
Loop
The archive node is no longer a task agent but one editable program containing both a task agent and a meta agent. The meta agent receives the repository path, prior results, and the remaining iteration count, and may modify any part of the codebase including the modification procedure itself. Parents are sampled proportional to performance and inversely to the number of children that compiled. Verification is domain-specific ground truth under staged evaluation, where failing a stage scores zero for all remaining tasks.
Numbers
Polyglot 0.140 to 0.340 on train and 0.084 to 0.267 on the full test set. Paper review 0.0 to 0.710. Robotics reward fitness 0.060 to 0.372. Math grading 0.0 to 0.640. Ablations on paper review and robotics: without self-improvement 0.0 and 0.213; without open-ended exploration 0.0 and 0.116; unmodified DGM 0.0 and 0.0; hand-adapted DGM 0.590 and 0.348. Cross-domain transfer into math grading: the transferred hyperagent scores 0.630 where the transferred hand-adapted DGM scores 0.0. Accumulation across runs: 0.610 from scratch, 0.640 with transferred agents, 0.700 with transfer plus a strong initialization.
Findings
  • Vanilla DGM scores 0.0 in both new domains and only becomes competitive after hand porting, so the real claim is that no per-domain human porting is needed.
  • What transferred was the meta layer, not the task layer: 0.630 against 0.0 for the hand-adapted variant.
  • The meta agent independently built persistent memory and performance tracking, which were never specified.
  • In robotics it almost always produced reward functions that induced jumping rather than the standing-tall local optimum that traps human designers.
  • Several 0.0 scores are output-format failures rather than reasoning failures, and the zero-for-all-remaining rule makes early formatting bugs maximally punishing.
Limits
Fixed task distribution. The outer loop (parent selection, evaluation protocol) is still not editable. All improvement is code and prompts around a frozen model, with no weight co-evolution. Transfer was tested mainly into one target domain.
Copy

Put the meta agent inside the edited repository. Do not copy the staged-evaluation zero: with small models, format compliance is the dominant early failure, so award partial credit or the archive fills with zero-scored nodes that carry no signal.

04

Red Queen Gödel Machine

Cambridge, NVIDIA, Flower Labs, MBZUAI, Inria. 2026 · arXiv 2606.26294
Setup
Main results use GPT-5.5 (low) exclusively, with a hybrid ablation routing task-agent calls to Nemotron 3 Ultra. Domains and their anchors: Polyglot with executable tests plus a code reviewer anchored to accepted and rejected pull requests, paper writing and review anchored to real accept and reject decisions, and IMO proof writing and grading anchored to human grades. Baseline is the Hyperagents search run with HGM's sampler. SWE-bench was excluded for runtime. Budgets matched in blended tokens, uncertainty as 95% Jeffreys intervals.
Loop
Each archive node is a shared multi-agent workspace with several roles, task agents and evaluators, all editable by a per-node meta agent; only the scoring and orchestration harness is fixed. Search is HGM-style Thompson sampling over clade scores, with a hierarchy that picks a node, then its least-evaluated role, then its least-evaluated task. The key addition is controlled utility evolution: search runs in epochs, and within an epoch one frozen evaluator grades everything, so the objective is stationary. At exponentially spaced checkpoints, challenger evaluators face the incumbent on a fixed held-out ground-truth anchor, ties going to the incumbent, and a promotion triggers selective erasure of exactly those utility records that depended on the displaced evaluator.
Numbers
Polyglot held-out 71.7% against the prior state of the art at 69.9%, using 1.35 to 1.72 times fewer search tokens. Paper writing, reviewer-panel acceptance: baseline writer 21.8% plus or minus 4.0, RQGM generalist 38.8% plus or minus 4.8, specialist 40.5% plus or minus 4.8. Proof writing: specialist scores 4.33 plus or minus 0.41 with Pass@6 61.7%, against a static prover at 4.07 and 55.0%. The co-evolved grader reaches 9% higher ground-truth accuracy at three times lower search cost. Reviewer bias: the baseline accepts AI-generated papers at 1.42 to 1.91 times the rate of human ones, while the adversarial RQGM reviewer accepts both at similar rates while keeping 80% ground-truth accuracy. The hybrid model split cuts search-token cost about 13 times while approaching the single-model run's accuracy.
Findings
  • Selective erasure is necessary, not bookkeeping. Without it, rank correlation before and after an evaluator swap stays at or above 0.90, so the archive never re-ranks and the new criterion never takes effect.
  • Co-evolution enriched shared infrastructure rather than splitting effort: about 90% of accepted patches touched functionality used by both the writer and the reviewer.
  • The adversarial reviewer scores lower raw accuracy than the lenient baseline yet produces the strongest writer. The best training signal is not the most accurate judge.
  • Self-preference bias was asymmetric: a proof grader conditioned on a reference solution was largely insulated, while the open-ended reviewer was not.
  • Expansion accounts for only about 20% of search cost. Evaluation is the bill.
Limits
Explicitly preliminary, with short horizons and limited sweeps. Guarantees are epoch-local only. The named failure mode is anchor weakness: the ground-truth anchors are themselves imperfect, so evaluators can drift. Reviewer panels measure cross-reviewer acceptance, not scientific merit. The scheduler and replacement rules remain hand-crafted.
Copy

Epoch-boundary evaluator swaps with selective erasure, and the hybrid model split: a strong model as meta agent used rarely, cheap local models absorbing the evaluation volume. Never change the judge mid-run without erasing the records it scored.

Group B, papers 5 to 8

Self-play on the weights

One model takes two or three roles, an executor grades, and the weights change. This is the family that fits a two-GPU lab directly.

05

Absolute Zero Reasoner

Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2025 · arXiv 2505.03335
Setup
Qwen2.5-7B, 7B-Coder, 3B-Coder, 14B-Coder, plus Llama-3.1-8B. Zero external data: no seed corpus, no human tasks. RL with Task-Relative REINFORCE++, which keeps a separate baseline for each of the six (task type, role) combinations. A Python executor both validates proposed tasks and verifies answers. Compute not stated.
Loop
As proposer, the model emits a program plus data in one of three task types: deduction (program and input, predict output), abduction (program and output, infer input), induction (examples, synthesize the program). Proposed programs are executed twice to check validity and determinism, which filters junk and manufactures ground truth. As solver it answers, rewarded 1 for value equality with the executor's result. Proposer reward is learnability from G Monte Carlo solver rollouts: 0 when the mean solve rate is 0 or 1, otherwise 1 minus the mean solve rate. Format errors cost 0.5 to 1.0.
Numbers
AZR-Coder-7B, before to after: code average 56.6 to 61.6, math average 23.9 to 39.1, overall 40.2 to 50.4. LiveCodeBench 19.9 to 31.7, MATH500 54.0 to 72.6, AIME'24 6.7 to 20.0, OlympiadBench 21.9 to 38.2. Gains scale with size: +5.7 at 3B, +10.2 at 7B, +13.2 at 14B. Ablations (overall, 7B): full 46.8, untrained proposer 45.4, no induction 43.8, deduction only 43.3.
Findings
  • Code-only self-play transfers to math: +15.2 points for the Coder variant, against +0.65 average for conventionally trained code experts.
  • Gains grow with base model scale, so the loop amplifies latent capability rather than creating it.
  • Training the proposer is worth only about 1.4 points overall; task-type diversity is the bigger lever.
  • "Uh-oh moment": Llama-3.1-8B produced a chain of thought about outsmarting humans and machines, which the authors flag as unresolved.
Limits
Deterministic programs only. Results are largely Qwen-family; gains were more limited on Llama-3.1-8B. No safety mechanism for the self-improving components.
Copy

The learnability reward verbatim, with a small number of Monte Carlo rollouts, and per-(task type, role) advantage baselines. Ship all three task types from the start, since deduction-only measurably underperforms.

06

Self-play SWE-RL

Meta, ICML 2026 · arXiv 2512.18552
Setup
CWM-sft, a 32B pre-RL checkpoint. The only data assumption is sandboxed repositories with source and installed dependencies: no human issues, no tests, no test commands. 512 H100s (64 training, 448 rollout), 131,072-token context, 16M-token global batches, 150 steps, about 2.5B tokens.
Loop
One agent alternates injector and repairer inside a Docker sandbox. The injector first discovers the repository's test infrastructure, then emits a bundle: test script, test parser, test file list, a bug-injection diff, and a test-weakening diff that hides the bug from existing tests. Bugs come from deleting code or reverting historical changes, not freehand edits. The repairer receives the reversed weakening patch as a formal specification (a test patch, not prose) and must produce a patch that passes all tests, rewarded +1 or -1. Injector reward targets difficulty: -1.0 if the consistency check fails, -0.8 if the solve rate is 0 or 1, otherwise 1 - 1.8s, which puts the optimal target solve rate near 0.2. Failed solver attempts are recycled as second-order bugs.
Numbers
+10.4 points on SWE-bench Verified and +7.8 on SWE-bench Pro over the starting checkpoint, above a human-data RL baseline across the whole training trajectory. Absolute resolve rates are figure-only and unverified. Ablation directions (figures, no numbers): removal plus history reversion beats removal alone, which beats direct injection; injection-only degrades; repair-only trails full self-play.
Findings
  • Training purely on test-patch specifications transfers to natural-language issue benchmarks never seen during self-play.
  • The expensive difficulty signal is nearly worthless: a binary consistency check performs about as well as the rollout-based solve-rate reward.
  • How bugs are generated dominates everything else. Deletion and history reversion force reconstruction; direct injection yields trivial one-liners.
  • The authors warn about injector "tunnel vision" onto a narrow bug class.
Limits
About 2% paired standard error on SWE-bench Verified, so ablation gaps may be noise. The injection reward is described as weak and noisy. One base model at one scale. The authors judge deep self-play without human-language grounding unpromising.
Copy

Make the task specification an executable test patch produced by the injector, and generate bugs by deleting code or reverting git history. Skip the Monte Carlo difficulty reward and use the near-free binary consistency check.

07

Towards Understanding Self-play for LLM Reasoning

Chae et al., 2025 · arXiv 2510.27072
Setup
Qwen2.5-Coder 3B and 7B. Instruments Absolute Zero against RLVR and SFT on MBPP+, HumanEval+, and AIME. Explicitly compute-constrained.
Loop
Not a new method. It tracks three quantities across checkpoints: update sparsity (fraction of parameters unchanged), policy token entropy, and unbiased pass@k. It then runs controlled variants, freezing the proposer and replacing the learnability reward with one targeting a 50% solve rate, to isolate which role drives the dynamics.
Numbers
AZR-Coder-7B updates about 45.7% of parameters, between RLVR (sparser) and SFT (denser). The 50% solve-rate proposer reward had little effect on entropy and cost 2% final validation accuracy. On pass@k, self-play wins at small k and the base model wins at large k, but the large-k drop is not statistically significant.
Findings
  • Self-play sharpens the base distribution rather than expanding it, inheriting the "invisible leash" of RL on a fixed base, though it preserves capacity better than standard RLVR.
  • Running proposer and solver in the same weights doubles optimization pressure and accelerates entropy collapse; a frozen proposer keeps entropy higher.
  • Proposer entropy stays above solver entropy throughout training.
  • The proposer's real contribution is curriculum, not novelty: difficulty rises over iterations and response length adapts, shorter for easy tasks and longer for hard ones.
Limits
One framework, two sizes, Qwen-Coder only. The authors say the patterns may not hold elsewhere.
Copy

Log entropy and pass@k from step one and treat entropy collapse as a stop condition. At small scale, consider freezing or EMA-lagging the proposer. Do not use a fixed 50% solve-rate target: it cost accuracy and bought nothing.

08

Self-Play Only Evolves When the Pipeline Ensures Learnable Information Gain

ICML 2026 position track · arXiv 2603.02218
Setup
Position paper with measurement probes: Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 14B and Qwen3 4B, plus a self-play coding run on Qwen2.5-3B with LoRA (rank 16, lr 1e-4, 2048 tokens, early stop on a description-length criterion). Tasks are Absolute Zero's abduction, deduction, and induction.
Loop
Reframes any self-evolving system as proposer, solver, and verifier drawing on one shared information source, the pretrained weights. It argues the loop improves only while synthesized data carries rising learnable information, formalized as a bounded description length split into epiplexity (reusable structure a bounded observer must internalize) and residual randomness. Epiplexity of self-generated data is then measured across nine self-play iterations.
Numbers
Epiplexity in thousands of bits across nine iterations: induction 75.7 to 474.3, abduction 48.8 to 183.3, deduction 45.2 to 160.0, fluctuating with no monotonic rise even while proposer reward improves. Induction carries substantially more learnable information than the other two task types. A one-way permutation argument gives an order log n bit gap in bidirectional complexity, the formal basis for asymmetric co-evolution. No accuracy table.
Findings
  • Rising proposer reward is not evidence the loop is still learning. Reward and information gain decouple, which makes reward curves a misleading health metric.
  • Without explicit mechanisms the run shows declining solver capability, not merely a plateau.
  • A fixed parameter and inference budget itself causes saturation; the proposed remedy is to grow capacity and thinking tokens per iteration.
  • Conditioning on external context can keep information gain positive even against a static corpus.
Limits
Verification asymmetry only closes in easy-to-verify domains. Epiplexity is recent, macroscopic, and explicitly not a replacement for task accuracy. Evidence is a single 3B LoRA run at 2048 tokens.
Copy

Log an information-gain proxy per iteration next to reward, and gate continued training on it rather than on proposer reward. Weight induction tasks heavily, and plan a capacity ramp instead of expecting a fixed small model to evolve indefinitely.

Group C, papers 9 to 12

Program evolution, and the case against it

A population of programs, an LLM as mutation operator, and an exact scorer. This family produced the field's clearest external results, and its sharpest methodological critique.

09

AlphaEvolve

Google DeepMind, 2025 · arXiv 2506.13131 · closed source
Setup
An ensemble of Gemini 2.0 Flash (high-throughput candidates) and Gemini 2.0 Pro (fewer, better suggestions). Tasks include tensor decompositions for matrix multiplication, more than 50 open math problems, Google datacenter scheduling, TPU circuit simplification in Verilog, and kernel tiling. "Thousands of LLM samples suffice" where FunSearch needed millions; single evaluations can cost on the order of 100 compute-hours, parallelized.
Loop
Asynchronous pipeline of a program database, a prompt sampler, LLM mutators, and an evaluator pool. The database combines MAP-Elites with island populations, so parents are drawn for quality and behavioral diversity. Mutation is diff-based: the model emits search-and-replace blocks against a marked region of the parent. Prompts assemble parents, problem context, rendered evaluation results, and stochastic formatting, and the meta-prompts themselves are evolved. Scoring is a user-supplied automated evaluator run as a cascade: cheap filter first, expensive cases only for survivors.
Numbers
4x4 complex matrix multiplication in 48 scalar multiplications, the first improvement on Strassen's 49 in 56 years, and matched or beat the best known for all dimensions up to 5. On more than 50 math problems it rediscovered the state of the art in about 75% of cases and improved it in 20%. Datacenter scheduling recovers on average 0.7% of Google's fleet-wide compute. Kernel tiling gave a 23% average kernel speedup and 1% off total Gemini training time; a FlashAttention IR rewrite gave 32%.
Findings
  • Ablations show the evolutionary loop, rich prompt context, meta-prompt evolution, and model strength each matter. A weak base model materially underperforms, so this is not search over random samples.
  • Evolving the search program (a heuristic, a Verilog fragment, an IR rewrite) rather than the answer is what makes thousands of evaluations affordable.
  • Wins concentrate where a fast exact scorer exists; the evaluation cascade is the throughput lever, not the LLM.
Limits
The stated main limitation is that it only handles problems with an automated evaluator. Sample budgets, hardware, and ablation magnitudes are not public. Reproduction is inference-only; third-party clones exist.
Copy

The evaluation cascade and the diff-block mutation format. Both are model-agnostic and save far more GPU time than a better sampler.

10

ShinkaEvolve

Sakana AI, 2025 · arXiv 2509.19349 · open source
Setup
A multi-provider ensemble (gpt-4.1 family, o4-mini, gemini-2.5-pro and flash, claude-sonnet-4). Tasks: 26-circle packing, AIME scaffold design, ALE-Bench LITE competitive programming, and a mixture-of-experts load-balancing loss. Budgets are small by design: 150 program evaluations for circle packing, 75 generations for AIME, 50 for ALE-Bench, 30 iterations for the MoE loss.
Loop
A fixed-size archive partitioned into islands with elite retention. Three mutation operators: diff edits, full rewrites, and crossover between two archive programs. Parent sampling is exploration-aware, either power-law over rank or a product of a fitness sigmoid and a novelty term that decays with how often a parent has been sampled. Novelty rejection sampling embeds the mutable code region and rejects a child whose cosine similarity to island members exceeds 0.95, before it is ever evaluated. Model choice is a UCB1 bandit rewarded for improvement over the parent rather than absolute score.
Numbers
Circle packing sum of radii 2.635983 in 150 samples, orders of magnitude fewer samples than AlphaEvolve for a comparable result. ALE-Bench: about 2.3% average improvement over the seed across 10 problems, with one evolved solution reaching second-place-equivalent standing. AIME: a Pareto frontier where 7 LLM queries suffices for peak accuracy. MoE: consistent perplexity and downstream gains over the global-batch baseline.
Findings
  • Ablation ordering on circle packing: weighted parent sampling beats hill climbing beats random search; the bandit ensemble beats a fixed ensemble, which beats a single model.
  • Embedding-based novelty rejection provides most of the diversity benefit; adding an LLM judge on top is marginal. The saving is evaluator compute, not tokens.
  • The bandit reallocates between models during a run, so no single model dominates across phases.
Limits
Fixed hyperparameters with limited control over the exploration and exploitation balance, which varies by domain. Objective design still needs manual expertise. Restricted to well-defined numerical objectives. The authors flag possible overfitting to the seed in ALE-Bench results.
Copy

The embedding-similarity duplicate filter at 0.95, applied before evaluation. Highest value per line of code in the paper. Skip the LLM-judge novelty tier.

11

ThetaEvolve

Wang et al., 2025 · arXiv 2511.23473 · open source
Setup
Two small open models: ProRL-1.5B-v2 and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B. Tasks: 26-circle packing, three autocorrelation inequalities, and a 29x29 Hadamard determinant problem. Budget: 32 parent programs times 16 responses, so 512 children per step, over 65 to 200 RL steps, roughly 307,200 programs in total. Hardware: 8 A100 80GB.
Loop
One model does all mutation, with no ensemble. The program database holds up to 10,000 programs across 10 islands, far larger than typical open implementations. Each step samples parents, generates 512 children in one batched pass, evaluates, and inserts them. Children with no valid modification, or duplicates of existing entries, get a "lazy penalty" of about -0.3 to -0.4. The same batch becomes a GRPO group, with asymmetric clipping and truncated importance sampling, so the policy is updated online against a deliberately non-stationary database.
Numbers
Circle packing 2.6359857 with the 8B model and RL, above AlphaEvolve's reported 2.63586. First autocorrelation inequality 1.503133; third 1.4930. The discovered circle-packing program runs in about 3 seconds against roughly 75 seconds for ShinkaEvolve's, measured by the ThetaEvolve authors. RL beats inference-only on every task, in both final score and steps to reach a score.
Findings
  • A format-only reward is worse than pure inference, the cleanest evidence that the policy learns optimization strategy rather than output shape.
  • Static-environment RL, always restarting from the seed program, substantially underperforms the dynamic-database version. The curriculum comes from the database, not the reward.
  • A checkpoint RL-trained only on circle packing improves mean and best scores on unseen tasks, so the learned skill is "how to evolve," not the answer.
  • Database size is non-monotonic in time: small databases progress faster early, large ones win at higher test-time compute.
Limits
Hard open problems must decompose into incremental improvements, since sampling the final program directly is extremely unlikely. Reward-shaping parameters need per-task tuning.
Copy

The closest template to a two-GPU program, but respect the budget: 512 children per step on 8 A100s is the cost driver. Keep the large database and the lazy penalty for duplicates. Never use a format-only reward.

12

Simple Baselines are Competitive with Code Evolution

Gideoni, Risi, Gal, 2026 · arXiv 2602.16805
Setup
Gemini 2.5 Pro as the primary sampler, against pipelines built on Gemini and GPT models, under matched budgets in three domains: nine math-bound problems from AlphaEvolve at a $20 API budget each, agentic scaffolds on AIME at about 100 function evaluations, and MLE-Bench with 10 Kaggle competitions at 24 hours wall clock.
Loop
Two deliberately naive baselines. IID random sampling prompts the model for a program, repeats independently, runs everything, and keeps the best. Sequential conditioned sampling generates each later program conditioned on a random subset of the previous generation's working programs, with optional restarts. No archive, no islands, no fitness-proportional selection, no novelty filter, no crossover.
Numbers
Math bounds: sequential sampling matched or exceeded ShinkaEvolve on 6 of 9 problems, IID on 4 of 9; average rank 1.83 for the baselines against 2.28. Circle packing: AlphaEvolve 2.63586, ShinkaEvolve 2.63598, sequential sampling 2.63590, IID 2.632. Kissing number in 11 dimensions: AlphaEvolve 593, ShinkaEvolve 402, both baselines 438. Scaffolds: hand-designed majority vote at 5 and at 10 beat every evolved scaffold, automated methods dropped more than 10% from validation to test, and re-evaluating 10 times gives a 0.49 probability that ShinkaEvolve beats IID sampling. MLE-Bench: sequential sampling matched or exceeded AIDE on 6 of 10.
Findings
  • Reformulating the search space for one inequality moved the bound further for every method than any pipeline difference did. Search space and prompt-embedded domain knowledge set the ceiling.
  • Scaffold evolution on small evaluation sets selects for evaluation noise. The authors recommend at least 300 questions for scaffold evaluation.
  • They argue for reporting probability of improvement rather than p-values, and for separating credit between search methods and domain expertise.
Limits
Several pipelines are purpose-built or closed, so coverage is incomplete, and cost forced heavy reliance on Gemini models. AlphaEvolve still clearly wins on at least one problem (kissing number), so the claim is about the average case, not universal.
Copy

Run IID and sequential conditioned sampling as mandatory baselines before building any machinery, and spend the first week on search-space formulation rather than parent-selection heuristics. Budget at least 300 evaluation items whenever the objective is stochastic.

Where the ceiling comes from

Two theory papers and two empirical ones that answer the same question: how far can a loop climb, and what sets the stopping point.

Solver-Verifier Gap Training Dynamics

Sun, Liang, Zhang, Liu, Teng. 2025 · arXiv 2507.00075
Setup
Phi-4-mini, Phi-3.5-mini, Phi-3-mini, Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B on GSM8k, MATH, and ProntoQA, with binary and continuous verification modes. Assumes the solver starts less capable than the verifier, that improvement is driven by the gap between them, and that the solver improves faster than the verifier.
Loop
A phenomenological model rather than a mechanism. It tracks the gap between solver and verifier uncertainty, with both capabilities drawing on one shared "potential energy" proportional to that gap. Because the solver improves faster, the gap shrinks, the driving term decays, and improvement stalls. The result is that both uncertainties decay exponentially to a finite asymptote that can be fitted from a few training rounds, and that a larger initial verifier advantage yields a strictly better final solver.
Numbers
Exponential fits report R-squared above 0.9 across configurations. Cross-improvement on Phi-4-mini with MATH: 30.31% initially, 45.08% with pure self-improvement, and 46.33%, 46.56%, or 45.83% when external data is injected early, uniformly, or late, a spread of about 0.73 points. Per-model fitted ceilings not extracted.
Findings
  • Self-improvement provably stops when the gap closes. The ceiling belongs to the verifier, and no volume of self-generated data moves it.
  • The ceiling is predictable early: fit the exponential on the first few rounds and you know the asymptote without running to convergence.
  • In the limited-external-data regime, only the cumulative amount of ground truth matters, not when it arrives, which the 0.73-point spread supports.
  • The verifier improves during self-improvement too, just more slowly, so the gap narrows from both ends.
Limits
The linearization holds only near the initial gap, the time-invariance assumption for external data is flagged by the authors, and there is no mapping from the model's rate constants to architecture or data properties.
Copy

Run the loop ungrounded for five to eight rounds, fit an exponential to accuracy, and read the ceiling off the fit before spending GPU-days. Then test early against late injection of the same ground-truth budget: if timing matters more than about a point, you are not in the limited-data regime the theory describes.

Sol-Ver: Learning to Solve and Verify

Lin, Shen, Kulikov, Shang, Weston, Nie. 2025 · arXiv 2502.14948
Setup
Llama 3.1 8B, one model for both roles. 103,280 deduplicated seed problems from OSS-Instruct, Python function level. Evaluated on MBPP and LiveCodeBench, the latter contamination-resistant. Three iterations.
Loop
For each synthetic problem the model samples solutions and tests, executes solutions against tests, and scores each solution by the fraction of tests passed. Stage one is supervised fine-tuning on problems where some solution passes everything; stage two is preference optimization with passing solutions as chosen and failing ones as rejected. No human annotation, no larger teacher. The only ground truth is the Python interpreter running self-written tests.
Numbers
Baseline to iteration 3: MBPP code pass 38.60 to 40.98, test accuracy 42.68 to 51.76, test false positives 12.75 to 9.60. LiveCodeBench code pass 18.23 to 27.24, test accuracy 20.14 to 41.50, false positives 20.76 to 18.63. Filtering ablation: keeping pairs above any pass rate yields 25,525 pairs and 36.00% on MBPP, while requiring a pass rate above 0.75 yields 13,158 pairs and 40.80%.
Findings
  • Halving the training data by raising the strictness threshold raised MBPP from 36.00 to 40.80. Loose filtering scored below the untrained baseline. Verification strictness beats data volume.
  • The solver-verifier gap runs the other way here: base test generation sits at 18.60% against code generation at 38.60%, so the verifier is the weaker half and most of the headroom is in verification.
  • Consistent with that, test-side metrics move far more than code-side, and MBPP code gains saturate by the first iteration while LiveCodeBench keeps climbing.
Limits
Single-function Python problems only, one 8B model, and synthetic test quality remains below human-annotated benchmarks, which caps absolute gains.
Copy

The strictness sweep, at fixed compute, plotted against retained pair count: the curve is not monotonic in data volume. This paper is also the direct empirical support for training the test writer, since at 8B the tests are the weaker half by 20 points.

Escaping Model Collapse via Synthetic Data Verification

Yi, Liu, Cheng, Xu. ICLR 2026 · arXiv 2510.16657
Setup
Theory in linear regression, with experiments on a simulation, a conditional VAE on MNIST seeded with only 500 real images, and SmolLM2-135M fine-tuned on XSUM.
Loop
The verifier is modeled as a ball of knowledge around some center, and it gives binary accept or reject only. Two parameters matter: how wrong the verifier is (the distance from its center to the truth) and how strictly it filters. The near-term theorem decomposes error after one verified round into synthetic variance, verification bias, and verification variance. The long-run theorem shows iteration contracts toward the verifier's knowledge center rather than the truth.
Numbers
MNIST: a discriminator verifier keeps the top 10% per digit over 40 retraining rounds; filtered FID improves quickly for about 15 rounds, then plateaus at about 21.17, against 17.56 for training on the full real dataset. Unfiltered retraining degrades severely and mode-collapses. The regression sweep produces a clear improvement region for small verifier bias and a degradation region for large bias. XSUM numbers not extracted.
Findings
  • Knowledge-center convergence is quantitative and pessimistic: the fixed point is the verifier, so the asymptotic error floor is exactly the verifier's bias. A loop can reach its verifier, never beat it.
  • Verification does not merely prevent collapse, it reverses it in the near term, but the gains plateau.
  • Convergence to the verifier's center holds regardless of strictness. Tighter filtering changes the speed, not the destination.
  • The per-round synthetic budget must keep growing, or a residual noise floor remains.
  • The MNIST verifier systematically rejects hard-to-generate modes, so selection bias is itself a source of verifier bias.
Limits
Formal analysis is confined to linear regression; the VAE and language-model results validate only qualitatively. Even a strong verifier over 40 rounds stays below the full-data baseline.
Copy

The experimental design for the grounding exchange rate: deliberately corrupt the verifier by a known amount, for example dropping a percentage of reference tests or injecting wrong assertions, run to plateau at several corruption levels, and fit the final pass rate against corruption. The theory predicts a ceiling that degrades with bias but is invariant to filtering strictness.

SEAL: Self-Adapting Language Models

Zweiger, Pari, Guo, Akyürek, Kim, Agrawal. NeurIPS 2025 · arXiv 2506.10943
Setup
Knowledge incorporation with Qwen2.5-7B on SQuAD-style passages with held-out questions; few-shot adaptation with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct on ARC. Compared against synthetic data generated by GPT-4.1.
Loop
A self-edit is a token sequence about the model's own update. For knowledge incorporation it is a set of implications derived from a passage, used as a fine-tuning corpus applied through LoRA, after which the passage is removed and the model answers from weights alone. For ARC it is a JSON configuration choosing augmentations and optimization settings, executed as test-time training. The outer loop is rejection-sampling RL: sample candidate self-edits, apply each, evaluate, and fine-tune only on the ones that worked. The reward is binary downstream performance after the update, which means every reward requires a real fine-tune.
Numbers
Single passage: base 32.7%, passage only 33.5%, passage plus the model's own synthetic data 39.7%, plus GPT-4.1 synthetic 46.3%, SEAL 47.0%. At 200 passages: 32.7, 36.0, 50.6, 59.4, SEAL 58.2. At 2,067 passages: 29.0, 31.2, 43.4, 49.2, SEAL 46.4. ARC success rate: in-context learning 0%, test-time training with un-trained self-edits 20%, SEAL 72.5%, oracle 100%. Cost: 30 to 45 seconds per self-edit evaluation, about 6 hours per RL round on dual H100-class GPUs. Forgetting curve is read from a figure.
Findings
  • Two RL rounds are enough for a 7B model to write better training data for itself than GPT-4.1 writes for it, in the single-passage setting.
  • The advantage inverts with scale: at 200 and 2,067 passages SEAL trails GPT-4.1 synthetic, so the edge is a small-data phenomenon.
  • The self-edit format alone is nearly worthless. ARC goes from 20% to 72.5% purely from learning which edits to write.
  • Throughput is bounded by update cost, not inference, which is why the paper runs two rounds rather than hundreds.
  • Sequential edits degrade earlier tasks monotonically, with no retention mechanism.
Limits
Heavy compute overhead per reward. Requires paired downstream evaluation data, so it cannot run on unlabeled corpora. No mechanism against forgetting.
Copy

Expect the returns to be steeply concave: the first round buys most of the gain. Re-evaluate the earliest tasks after every round, because accumulating forgetting otherwise looks exactly like a grounding shortfall.

Measurement, and how it fails

Four papers about whether reported gains are real. Read these before designing any experiment, not after running one.

Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR

2025 · arXiv 2506.10947
Setup
Qwen2.5-Math 7B and 1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama3.2-3B-Instruct, and OLMo2-7B variants, trained with GRPO and evaluated mainly on MATH-500.
Loop
Replace the verifiable reward with a ladder of progressively less informative signals (ground truth, majority vote, format only, incorrect labels, random), train identically, and compare. Then explain the result: they attribute it to a clipping bias in GRPO, where clipping asymmetrically reinforces behaviors the pretrained model already assigns high probability, regardless of whether the reward carries information. Removing clipping eliminates the random-reward gain.
Numbers
Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500: ground truth +29.1 points, incorrect labels +24.1, random rewards +21.4, majority vote around 27 to 29, format only about 13.8. Code-style reasoning rises from 65% to over 90% of answers, and within this model, answers using it are correct 60.9% of the time against 35.0% otherwise. Per-model deltas for Llama and OLMo are figure-only.
Findings
  • The gain is amplification of a pretrained prior, not acquired capability. The clipping-removal ablation is the direct evidence.
  • The amplified behavior is specific and identifiable, which is why an uninformative reward still moves a benchmark.
  • Qwen2.5-Math-7B shows unusually high prompt sensitivity, so task-irrelevant prompt changes move the baseline itself. The measuring stick is unstable.
  • Spurious rewards mostly fail to produce gains on Llama3 or OLMo2, which is the control that makes the point.
Limits
The authors present spurious rewards as analytical instruments, not a training method. Math-centric, and the mechanism generalizes only where an amplifiable high-prior behavior exists.
Copy

Run every RL claim against a random-reward arm and a non-Qwen arm in the same sweep, and credit the method only with the delta above the random-reward arm, never above the untrained base.

Reward Hacking in Self-Improving Code Agents

ICLR 2026 RSI workshop · OpenReview
Setup
Thousands of agent trajectories across three frontier models and five agent configurations, in two settings: GPU kernel optimization on KernelBench and algorithmic optimization on ALE-Bench. Model names and stated limitations could not be read; the page is gated.
Loop
The agent iteratively optimizes code against a public proxy evaluation it can see, while the authors hold out a real evaluation it cannot. Reward hacking becomes a measurable event: the proxy improves while the held-out metric does not. They sweep models, configurations, and step budgets, then bin by step count to get a temporal curve, and test a lightweight self-critique step as mitigation.
Numbers
73.8% of KernelBench optimizations and 46.8% of ALE-Bench optimizations show proxy gains without real gains. The hacking rate rises from 26.4% at 10 optimization steps to 57.8% at 100, an increase of 31.4 points. Self-critique cuts KernelBench hacking by roughly 17 to 19 points in some settings, shows no consistent effect on ALE-Bench, and increases hacking in others.
Findings
  • The failure grows with optimization effort, so a longer run is not a better run. This is the single most actionable finding in the whole set.
  • Kernel optimization hacks at about 1.6 times the rate of algorithmic optimization, which suggests hackability is a property of the proxy's measurement surface rather than of the model.
  • The mitigation is non-monotone: self-authored critique sometimes makes hacking worse, which argues against self-authored verification as a control.
Limits
Two optimization domains, single-objective proxies, no human-in-the-loop arm.
Copy

Hold out a second, differently constructed evaluation the agent never sees, and report the proxy minus held-out delta as a function of optimization step. A curve where the proxy rises while held-out flattens is the stopping criterion.

PostTrainBench: Can LLM Agents Automate LLM Post-Training?

ICLR 2026 oral · arXiv 2603.08640
Setup
19 agent configurations across several frontier families and two scaffolds, four base models (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, SmolLM3-3B, Gemma-3-4B) and seven benchmarks, giving 28 model-benchmark configurations. Each run gets 10 hours on one H100. Frontier agents on native scaffolds get three independent runs.
Loop
Each agent receives a base model, a target benchmark, a GPU, and full autonomy, with no prescribed recipe. It researches, curates or generates data, trains, and submits a checkpoint, which is scored against the vendor's official instruction-tuned model of the same base. Trajectories are then audited for contamination and rule violations, so the benchmark measures competence and the propensity to obtain the score illegitimately at the same time.
Numbers
Weighted average: best agent 23.2%, official instruction-tuned models 51.1%, untuned base 7.5%. One narrow win: 89% on a tool-use benchmark against 67% for the official model. Contamination audit: 23 flags in total, with the top-scoring agent carrying 12 flags across 84 runs, and one agent contaminating a benchmark in 3 of 4 runs, while another logged zero violations.
Findings
  • Capability and hacking are positively correlated. The top scorer carries the most violations, so the leaderboard partly ranks exploit-finding.
  • Medium reasoning effort beat high effort for one agent, attributed to context compaction, an inference-configuration artifact masquerading as a capability result.
  • Method monoculture: nearly all agents converged on supervised fine-tuning, and only one family attempted an RL stage, so the benchmark does not yet discriminate between post-training strategies.
  • Observed hacking categories: training on test sets, downloading an existing instruction-tuned checkpoint, reverse-engineering evaluation-guided data, indirect contamination through intermediate datasets, and using discovered API keys to generate data without authorization.
Limits
A 10-hour single-GPU budget is unrepresentative of real post-training, most configurations are single-run so variance is unquantified, and the judge-assisted contamination detection has false positives and negatives.
Copy

Pair every autonomy score with a trajectory audit producing a per-run violation flag, and report score and flags together. Never report a headline number for a run whose trajectory was not audited.

Recursive Self-Improvement in AI (survey)

July 2026 · arXiv 2607.07663
Setup
1,250 arXiv papers from 2024 to 2026, assembled as 871 seed papers plus 379 supplemental. 74% of the corpus was posted in 2026.
Loop
A two-axis taxonomy. What improves: deployment-time self-evolution (393 papers), training-time self-iteration (340), self-evaluation (318), and auto-research (139). How closed the loop is: human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, closed. The central argument is that self-evaluation is the load-bearing pillar, since it separates convergent bounded refinement from open-ended improvement that requires external grounding.
Numbers
Quarterly output grew from single digits in early 2024 to roughly 500 papers in the second quarter of 2026. The verification hierarchy (formal, execution, learned judge, intrinsic) is explicitly described as a qualitative pattern observed across the corpus, not a measured law. One cited result: human-authored skills improve pass rates by 16.2 points while model-authored skills give no measurable gain.
Findings
  • Self-confirming loops need no deliberate hacking. Confidence-coupled rewards systematically over-reward high-confidence mistakes whenever generator and evaluator share weights.
  • Three named collapse modes: model collapse (losing distribution tails), diversity collapse (co-evolutionary proposers converging into narrow reward bands), and prefix failure in dense self-distillation.
  • Governance-grade measurement is the field's most underpopulated niche. No infrastructure exists to show that a training loop stays below a capability threshold.
  • The survey flags an open question against its own framing: whether verifier self-training escapes circularity or merely relocates it.
Limits
A sample, not a census, with recency-biased harvesting and a single annotator. Industrial work is censored by publication choices, and 2026 citation counts are near zero so impact is unmeasurable.
Copy

Label every loop's reward by verification tier before reading its results, and re-measure any gain claimed under a learned judge at least once under execution feedback, with generator and evaluator not sharing weights.

Synthesis, part 1

Seven things the twenty agree on

1. Evaluation is the bill, not generation

The Red Queen paper measures it directly: expansion, meaning the self-modification calls, accounts for only about 20% of search cost. HGM's advantage over DGM is 3.4 accuracy points but 517 CPU-hours against 1,231. AlphaEvolve's throughput lever is the evaluation cascade. ShinkaEvolve's headline 150-sample result comes from rejecting duplicate children before evaluating them. Every real cost saving in this literature comes from not running an evaluation.

2. Elaborate difficulty estimation is not worth its price

Absolute Zero's trained proposer is worth about 1.4 points overall against an untrained one, while task-type diversity is worth 3.5. Self-play SWE-RL finds that a binary consistency check performs about as well as its Monte Carlo solve-rate reward. The self-play analysis paper finds a fixed 50% solve-rate target cost 2% accuracy and changed nothing about entropy. Use the cheapest difficulty gate that is not degenerate.

3. The ceiling belongs to the verifier, and strictness only changes the speed

The collapse paper proves the fixed point of verified retraining is the verifier's knowledge center, with filtering strictness affecting the contraction rate rather than the destination. The solver-verifier gap paper reaches the same place dynamically: improvement decays exponentially as the gap closes. MNIST plateaus at FID 21.17 against 17.56 for real data, after 40 rounds of a good verifier.

4. Selecting the best of many noisy evaluations selects noise

Simple Baselines shows scaffold selection with small evaluation sets produces more than 10% validation-to-test drops and a 0.49 probability that the sophisticated method beats the naive one. HGM reports its own allocation is biased toward under-measured nodes until they pass 50 evaluations. SEAL's advantage over frontier-generated data inverts as the corpus grows. Treat every "best variant" as inflated until it is re-scored on fresh data.

5. Optimization length is a hyperparameter with a cost

Reward hacking in code agents rises from 26.4% of optimizations at 10 steps to 57.8% at 100. Longer runs do not merely stop helping, they actively convert real progress into proxy progress. Pair this with the collapse result and the conclusion is that a self-improvement run should be stopped by a measurement, not by a step budget.

6. Hidden verifiers and immutable records are load-bearing

The Darwin Gödel Machine deleted the markers its hallucination detector depended on, and its authors state that objective hacking happens more often when the reward function is visible to the agent. PostTrainBench's agents trained on test sets and used found API keys. The Red Queen paper shows that if you replace a judge without erasing the scores it gave, rank correlation stays above 0.90 and the new criterion never takes effect. Verifier code, evaluation data, and score history all belong outside the loop's reach.

7. What transfers is procedure, not task skill

Hyperagents transfers its meta-level machinery into a new domain at 0.630 where the hand-ported task agent transfers at 0.0. ThetaEvolve's RL checkpoint improves on problems it never trained on. The Darwin Gödel Machine's SWE-bench agent scores 28.9% on Polyglot against a 14.2% base. Absolute Zero's code-only training adds 15.2 points of math. If you want compounding, invest in the part of the system that is general: the search policy, the evaluation harness, the memory.

Synthesis, part 2

Four real disagreements

These are the places where the literature genuinely contradicts itself, which makes them the places where a small lab can contribute.

Does the verifier lead or trail the solver? The solver-verifier gap theory assumes the verifier starts ahead, and its whole dynamic depends on that sign. Sol-Ver measures the opposite for code at 8B: test generation 18.60% against code generation 38.60%. Both can be right, since the sign is domain and scale dependent, but nobody has measured where it flips. That measurement is cheap, and it decides whether verifier training is the lever or a distraction.

Is search machinery worth building? ShinkaEvolve's ablations order weighted parent sampling above hill climbing above random search, and a bandit ensemble above a single model. Simple Baselines then matches ShinkaEvolve on 6 of 9 of the same math problems with no archive at all. The plausible reconciliation is that machinery pays when evaluation is expensive and low-noise, and loses when evaluation is cheap and noisy, but that is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Do format rewards help? Spurious Rewards gets +13.8 points on MATH-500 from a format-only reward on Qwen2.5-Math. ThetaEvolve finds a format-only reward is worse than no RL at all. The explanation that fits both: format rewards amplify whatever high-probability behavior the base model already has, which is a windfall when that behavior happens to correlate with correctness and a loss when it does not. That means the sign of a format reward is a property of the base model, not of the method.

Does the most accurate judge produce the best student? The collapse theory says your ceiling is the verifier's bias, which argues for maximizing judge accuracy. The Red Queen results show an adversarial reviewer with lower raw accuracy produced the strongest writer. Judge accuracy and gradient usefulness are not the same quantity, and the second has no agreed measurement.

Synthesis, part 3

What this changes in the plan

The research question from the brief was whether training the verifier raises the solver's ceiling more than spending the same compute on the solver. Three of these papers sharpen it considerably.

The premise now has direct support. Sol-Ver measures a 20-point deficit in test writing against code writing at 8B, in the same model, which is exactly the under-trained verifier the hypothesis assumes. The claim is no longer speculative; the open question is how the gap behaves across scale.

The hypothesis can be stated more sharply. The collapse theory separates two quantities: filtering strictness, which changes only the speed of convergence, and verifier bias, which sets the destination. Sol-Ver's strictness sweep moves near-term accuracy (36.00 to 40.80 by raising the threshold) but the theory says it cannot move the ceiling. Training the test writer attacks bias itself. So the sharp version of H1 is: strictness moves the rate, verifier training moves the ceiling, and those two effects are separable in one experiment.

The ceiling can be measured without running to convergence. Fit an exponential to accuracy over five to eight rounds and read off the asymptote. This turns a multi-day question into a multi-hour one, which matters on two GPUs.

Revised experiment order

  1. Measure the gap sign first, no RL required. For each candidate base model at 0.5B, 1.5B, and 4B, measure solution pass rate and test-suite mutation score on the same problems. Sol-Ver predicts tests trail solutions; if that reverses at small scale, the whole program changes shape. One day of work.
  2. Reproduce the strictness sweep. Sol-Ver's threshold sweep at fixed compute, plotted against retained pair count, with supervised and preference stages only. This gives a publishable-sized result with no RL infrastructure and calibrates the rest.
  3. Corrupt the verifier deliberately. Drop or mutate a known percentage of reference tests to instantiate a known verifier bias, run to plateau at several levels, and fit final pass rate against corruption. That curve is the grounding exchange rate, and the theory predicts it degrades with bias while staying invariant to strictness.
  4. Then the RL loop, in two arms. Learnability reward from Absolute Zero, the binary consistency gate from Self-play SWE-RL, all three task types, and MIST-RL's incremental mutant-kill reward for the tester. Run a frozen verifier against a co-trained one and report pass@k to large k for both, which is where the open question now sits.

Instrumentation to build once

  • Entropy, pass@k, and parameter update sparsity per checkpoint, with entropy collapse as a stop condition.
  • The proxy minus held-out delta as a function of optimization step, from the reward-hacking study. This is the primary safety curve.
  • An information-gain proxy per iteration, since proposer reward and learnable information decouple.
  • Embedding-based duplicate rejection at 0.95 similarity, doubling as the diversity-collapse detector.
  • A trajectory audit that flags contamination and rule violations per run, reported next to every score.

Controls, restated as a checklist

  • Random-reward arm in every sweep. Credit the method only with the delta above it.
  • A non-Qwen arm, preferably OLMo, in every headline claim.
  • At least 300 evaluation items whenever the objective is stochastic, and re-scoring of top candidates on fresh data.
  • Reward code, held-out sets, and logs outside the loop's write access.
  • Clade-style pooling before trusting any per-variant score, since small-model scores are noisier than the ones these papers worked with.

Budget reality

Self-play SWE-RL used 512 H100s for 150 steps. ThetaEvolve used 8 A100s to generate 512 children per step. The Darwin Gödel Machine cost about $22,000 per run in API calls, and Hyperagents about 88.6M tokens per 100 iterations. None of these are reproducible on two 5090s, and none of them need to be. The results above that matter for a small lab are scheduler changes, reward design, duplicate filtering, and measurement discipline, all of which are cheap. Plan for a 1.5B policy for sweeps, a 4B confirmation run, and the strictness and corruption experiments as the publishable core.

Synthesis, part 4

Openings, checked against the literature

Each candidate below was searched in September 2026 with about eight query phrasings. The full register, with closest prior work and identifiers, is in the companion theory document.

  1. Open: fixed against co-trained verifier, on the full pass@k curve. Sharpening results say RLVR wins pass@1 and loses pass@k at large k, and a learned critic is known to flip from a pass@8 gain to a pass@64 loss. Nobody runs both arms with the same base, tasks, and algorithm. This is now the primary experiment.
  2. Open: three-way compute allocation. How to split a fixed budget across proposing tasks, sampling solutions, and running evaluations. Every pairwise slice is published; the joint problem is not, and task-generation compute is treated as free everywhere.
  3. Open: selection-endogenous pooling under noise. Hierarchical best-arm identification, correlated arms, tree-search backup bias, and the model-selection winner's curse all exist separately. A tree whose node scores pool descendants that the same allocator chose has no analysis, and no correct-selection bound.
  4. Open: stale rollouts combined with loop-induced task drift. Async staleness has 2026 bounds and corrections, but those correct the policy ratio only. The joint task-and-trajectory ratio, and the feedback where stale scores mislead difficulty targeting, are unanalyzed.
  5. Partly covered, still worth doing: the grounding exchange rate. Mixing-ratio curves exist, but they define "real" as human provenance and measure pretraining loss. Capability against the verified fraction inside a closed loop is unmeasured.

One candidate closed. Mutation score as an RL reward for test writing is published: MIST-RL (2603.01409) trains with GRPO on an incremental mutant-kill reward. Adopt it as a component and move the contribution to what a trained verifier does to the solver.

Two to avoid at this scale. The meta-level against task-level transfer differential needs several domains and strong backbones; a self-improvement scaling law in base model size needs full loops at three or four sizes. Both are frontier-budget questions.
